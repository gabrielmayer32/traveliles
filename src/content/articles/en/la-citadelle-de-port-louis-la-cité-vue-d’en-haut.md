Rising above the bustle of Port Louis, its markets, busy streets and harbour, an imposing stone silhouette keeps watch over the capital. Perched on Petite Montagne, the Citadelle, or Fort Adelaide, offers a different perspective on the city, and on an important chapter in Mauritian history.

As you make your way uphill, Port Louis gradually reveals itself: its streets network, the Champ de Mars, the buildings of the city centre and the harbour beyond. From the ramparts, the panorama almost feels like a map unfolding before you, offering a single sweeping view of the city and its geography.

**A Fortress Built in a Time of Change**

Fort Adelaide was built by the British between 1834 and 1840, during the governorship of William Nicolay, at around 240 feet above sea level. It was named Adelaide in honour of the wife of King William IV.

The timing is significant. Mauritius was then going through a period of profound change. Slavery was abolished under British rule, while the arrival of indentured labourers was beginning to reshape the island’s social and economic landscape. Against this backdrop of political and social tension, the fortress was intended both to address external military concerns and to reinforce colonial authority over the capital.

Its construction tells another part of that story. The site brought together a remarkably diverse workforce, including British engineers and soldiers, craftsmen, prisoners, Indian indentured labourers and local workers, all contributing to the construction of this imposing structure.

**Basalt Walls Overlooking Port Louis**

Imposing without being ornate, the Citadelle is striking above all for its restraint. Its thick basalt walls, vaulted passages, large courtyard and historic artillery pieces all recall its original purpose: to observe, protect and control. Yet the fort was barely ever called upon to fulfil the military role for which it had been designed. By the time construction was completed in 1840, the threats that had justified its creation had already largely receded.

The Citadelle nonetheless remains one of the most important landmarks in Port Louis’ built heritage and is today listed among Mauritius’ national heritage sites.

**Seeing Port Louis Differently**

Perhaps it is today that the site reveals its full meaning.

From the ramparts, the experience is about more than the view. The position of Port Louis becomes easier to understand: a city set between mountains and sea, open to its harbour and surrounded by places that have each played a role in shaping the history of the capital.

Below, the city continues to move, trade and evolve. Above, the stone walls remain silent.

The Citadel creates a striking contrast: a fortress built to watch over a city that, nearly two centuries later, has expanded and continues to live all around it.
